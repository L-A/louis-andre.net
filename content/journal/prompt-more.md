"So has AI, like, affected your work?"

Every time, the question is asked gently, with a touch of worry in the tone. _Are you holding up?_

Work kind of remains what it is. Generative LLMs have _strongly_ changed how work comes to me, however, and how my relationships start with clients as well. I'm actually (still? for now?) optimistic about these changes.

## The protracted relationships

Not all clients _want_ to work with a service provider. Some of them never see the value of what I provide, but must work with me because my work is the bridge to something they need. If they could pay zero dollars, or maybe find the cheapest, worst provider possible just to have it done immediately, they would.

Sometimes, other factors[^0] force a client like this to work with me, and the value they gain from my work is kind of lost on them – they benefit because circumstances obligate them to receive that benefit. There's no moment where they gain the insight that an experienced designer has a positive outcome on the business or cause, and I myself have also failed to convince them. If you ask them, I'm in the way and they're doing everyone a favor by enduring the process.

These clients are sometimes a source of revenue, but mostly they're opportunities I want to filter out and turn down. Now, breathless AI marketing does the work of luring them away for me, and they leave me alone.

## Almost there

The type I _do_ have to filter out is the client who has been Marketed to, and has been given permission by the Very Well-Balanced People of LinkedIn to treat every discipline they don't understand as something that can be prompted to completion by typing the right words into a computer[^1].

They get started on something, and, wow! AI tools can certainly sprint to results that look like a completed process. But, after darting to a finished-looking app or website, it seems to them like they're not getting _quite_ the result they want. They fiddle around. The new result again looks final, but it's not exactly there. They aren't sure where to go from that. They contact me.

_We're almost there, like 80% done, and we'd like you to step in and get the project to the finish line._

You're 80% there? Sounds like you could prompt 20% more and be done!

I usually pick a more diplomatic way of saying it, but now that's the gist of my reply. With the protracted client, I _can't_ get them to understand my value, they're not interested in seeing it. With the almost-there client, they're open to understanding the limit of an AI-centric process.

So I invite that client to experience the wall they think I can magick away if I step in at the last minute. I need them to notice the fragile foundations of the work, to see that however much they want to move further than the dead end, that more time and effort leads to no additional progress. When a project's design is scaffolded on an AI-centric process, the distance from 80% to 100% is near-infinite.

Sometimes they turn into great clients, because after they've brought the attempt to its inevitable end, they come back with an understanding of where the possibilities fizzle out. We can start talking about what work that goes _beyond_ can do for them, and at that point they're well-equipped to decide if my contribution would be worth it.

[^0]: Factors like their developers screaming that _something_ must be done about the design, clients leaving because the app technically works, but _only technically_, etc.

[^1]: As it happens, many, many disciplines work exactly like this. What they have in common is the application of a skill.
